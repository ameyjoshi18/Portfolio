The first connection

One act, at growing scale.

Taking two things that can’t talk to each other, and making them talk. I’ve been doing it since school. What happened is on the left. What it turned out to mean is on the right.

v0.1

The cable

It’s late in Kodoli, and a boy is kneeling beside a computer that has never once been on the internet. In one hand, a Nokia. In the other, a USB cable. The town outside has no connection to offer him, so he is building his own.

Nokia PC Suite. A 2G signal. A progress bar that moves like a slow train. And then one webpage, loading on a screen that has only ever known Mario, IGI missions and Vice City.

One page. That was the entire achievement. It felt enormous.

I took two machines that couldn’t talk, and made them talk.

The rest of childhood built the pattern. Kodoli till 6th standard. Kolhapur from 7th, with Tata Photon dongles, then gaming cafés where I paid by the hour for Counter-Strike, then Dota, then snooker. Any game I touched, any system with rules, I got good at fast.

Systems simply made sense to me.

v0.9

Bharati Vidyapeeth

It’s results season, and a college clerk I’ve never met is logging into something my closest friend and I built. He doesn’t know us. He doesn’t need to. It just works.

That was our final-year project: an Office Automation System our own college put into production. Three years of building project after project with my co-founder had led to real people depending on our software.

A second connection: an idea on paper, talking to the real world.

We didn’t want jobs. We wanted a company.

v1.0 · 2018–19

Zenox goes live

A landlord hands over keys to a small apartment. We put a desk in it and call it an office. On paper, Zenox Technologies exists. In reality: two graduates and a room.

Year one brought no clients. So we built anyway. Websites, plugins, browser extensions, tools nobody had asked for. Then word of mouth did what advertising couldn’t. One client became two. Two became ten.

The boy who once smuggled the internet into his house through a phone cable now spent his days wiring other people’s businesses into it.

Same act. Bigger systems.

Our rule: never say “we can’t.” Learn it first. Then build it.

Connection lost · 2020

Incident 001

The phone rings, and it’s another cancellation. Then another. Our clients are small businesses, and small businesses are switching everything off at once.

COVID emptied the pipeline overnight. For the first time since that Nokia cable, the connections were failing faster than I could make them. We cut costs to the bone and held on. When the first lockdown lifted, we did something that made no sense on paper and perfect sense to us.

We bet on the thing we loved most. Gaming.

v1.5 · February 2021

RB Esports

Opening month. Every seat taken. The room glows the way the cafés of my school years used to glow, the ones where I paid by the hour to play Counter-Strike.

Except this time, I built the room.

RB Esports was everything I knew about hardware, networks and players, poured into one café in Kolhapur. The best in the city, we believed. It is still running.

The kid who paid at the counter was now behind it.

Connection lost · five months

Incident 002

There’s a notice on the café’s shutter, and it doesn’t come down for five months. Inside, the machines we borrowed money to buy sit in the dark. The EMIs don’t sit anywhere. They arrive every month, on time.

Quitting would have been easier. We paid, we waited, and when the doors opened we came back bigger: PCs, laptops and hardware sales at the café, university portals and larger platforms at Zenox.

Two shutdowns in two years. We held both times. That’s the line on my résumé no one can see.

v2.0 · late 2023

The refactor

I’m staring at a list of every project I’ve ever run, trying to understand why the work feels finished. The business is stable. And I’ve hit the ceiling of what Kolhapur can offer me.

Then the list answers back. On every single project, I was never exactly the coder, and never exactly the manager. I was the one in between.

Turning what the business meant into what the engineers built. Making both sides talk. There’s a name for that job.

Discovering business analysis wasn’t a career change. It was finally naming the thing I’d been doing since a Nokia and a USB cable.

The act needed bigger machines. There was one place they kept the biggest ones.

v2.5 · 2024

IDFC FIRST Bank

My first release at a bank is waiting. Not on code. The code is done. It’s waiting on business sign-off, compliance, security, audit, operations.

Software here isn’t shipped. It’s permitted.

On NRI-PIS, ASBA, Demat and API integrations, I learned the handshake rules of very large machines: what two systems must satisfy before they are allowed to speak, in a place where a missed defect has a price with real digits in it.

Consulting taught me to build. Banking taught me to build responsibly.

v3.0 · 2025

Fino. The cutover.

It’s the middle of an execution window, and a bank’s core is being replaced while the bank is still a bank. Months of planning compressed into hours. Business, engineering, QA, infrastructure, vendors, leadership. One sequence, no room for error.

The move from FIS to Finacle was the most demanding thing I’ve been part of. One system handing a living bank to another without dropping it. I loved every bit of it.

Great systems aren’t built by brilliant individuals. They’re built by teams that trust each other under pressure.

Then the rails themselves: AePS, money transfer, eKYC, micro-ATM, 3DS. A worker’s cash in one city becoming a buzz on a phone hundreds of kilometres away.

Twenty years earlier it was one webpage over 2G. Now the page has to load every single time.

v4.0 beta

Still connecting

I’m not chasing a designation. I’m chasing scale.

Zenox taught me to build from nothing. Banking taught me to build at scale. And the pattern hasn’t stopped. I’m building my own products now, and reaching for every new technology as it arrives. Not out of fear of being left behind. Because using what’s new to do extraordinary things is the whole point.

Still that kid with the cable.

Twenty years on. The systems just got bigger.
